What it is

A Filipino card game that plays like a real family party.

Everyone is trying to host the best salo-salo. You need three Food cards, three Family cards and three Fun cards on the table. The first to complete all nine shouts “Salo-Salo!” and wins.

The foodKare-kare, lumpia, sisig, halo-halo. The table has to be full before anyone can call it a party.
The familyNinang, Ate, Tita, Kuya. Every guest card is hand-drawn standing at your door, right before you say come in, sit down.
The funThe parol, the pabitin, the barrel man, the mahjong table. Atmosphere is non-negotiable.
The sabotageA Bagyo wipes out a whole category. A Brownout kills the Fun. Tita shows up with tsismis and somebody's guest walks out.
Easy to learn, hard to stop playing On your turn you draw one card and play one card. Kids pick it up in a round or two. Online orders also come with a printed quick-start, so lolos and lolas can follow along on paper.

Who it's for

Made for the table where the whole family sits.

Christmas, Noche Buena and New Year's

Something to do after the food and before the karaoke. It plays in under an hour, so the cousins, the titos and the kids can all get a round in.

Filipino American families teaching culture at home

The game was made by a Filipino American mom for her son. The Tagalog words are part of the play, so kids learn them by saying them out loud, not by studying them.

A gift for your tita, your barkada or the balikbayan box

A small, beautiful box that every Filipino recognizes the minute they open it. It makes a thoughtful Christmas gift, a hostess gift for a party, or a fun add-on for family back home.

Classrooms, Filipino clubs and community events

Teachers, FilAm student groups and cultural organizations use it to start conversations about family, values and language in a way that feels like play.

The culture

The rules come from how Filipino families actually live.

Every mechanic is something your family already does. That's why people laugh the first time they read the cards.

KapwaWelcome the unexpected guest instead of fighting them. Connection turns trouble into blessing.
Utang na LoobCall in a debt of gratitude, and the next two cards drawn come straight to you.
Mano PoThe strongest counter in the deck. Respect stops anything coming your way.
Filipino TimeNobody's guests can show up for two whole rounds.
Bahala Ka Sa Buhay MoReverse it entirely. Whoever came for you deals with it themselves.
Tita's TeaTita has the latest scoop, and somebody's guest walks out.

Questions

Good to know before you order.

Do I need to speak Tagalog to play?

No. The game is in English with Tagalog words woven into the cards, and the full rules include a glossary. Players who don't speak Tagalog pick the words up as they play.

How many people can play, and how long does it take?

Two to five players, ages 8 and up. A game takes about 30 to 45 minutes.

Is it a good gift?

Yes. It comes in a wraparound tuck box with original illustration on every card, and it fits easily in a stocking, a gift bag or a balikbayan box.

Where can I buy it?

Order online and it ships from Cerritos, California with USPS Ground Advantage. Shipping is $5.95 for one game and free on two or more, US addresses only. You can also find it at Seven Syllables Coffee in Cerritos while supplies last.

Can I try it before I buy?

Yes. There's a free browser demo where you can play solo against Tita AI or pass the phone for two players. No download needed.

Who made it?

Salo Salo Sabotage was created and illustrated by Ren Dela Cruz of Salita, a Filipino American author and educator. It's printed in a small run in Richmond, California on FSC-certified recycled paper with soy-based inks.
